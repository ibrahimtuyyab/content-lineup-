  - q: Can I do keyword research entirely for free?
    a: Yes. Google Search Console shows what you already rank for, autocomplete and People Also Ask show how people phrase things, and the results page itself shows what Google thinks the search means. Paid tools mostly buy you speed and competitor data, not access to something otherwise unavailable.
  - q: How accurate are search volume numbers?
    a: Treat them as an order of magnitude, not a measurement. Different tools report very different figures for the same term because they all model from incomplete data. The difference between 50 and 5,000 searches a month is meaningful; the difference between 320 and 390 is noise.
  - q: What are long tail keywords and why do they matter?
    a: Longer, more specific searches with lower volume and much clearer intent — "how to schedule instagram posts for multiple clients" rather than "instagram". They matter because a small site can realistically rank for them, they convert better since the need is explicit, and enough of them add up to serious traffic.
  - q: How many keywords should one page target?
    a: One primary search plus the handful of close variations that mean the same thing. A page about scheduling social posts naturally covers "how to schedule social media posts" and "social media scheduling tool" — that is one topic phrased differently, not two targets. Two genuinely different intents need two pages.
  - q: Should I target keywords my competitors already rank for?
    a: Only if you can do the job noticeably better, or answer a version of the question they answer badly. Publishing a slightly worse copy of the page currently in position one is the most common wasted effort in content marketing.

[answer]
Do keyword research in four passes, all free: pull what you already rank for from Google Search Console, expand it with autocomplete and People Also Ask, read the actual results page for each candidate to judge what Google thinks the search means, then keep only the searches where your page could plausibly be the best result. Volume matters far less than intent and winnability.
[/answer]

Keyword research has an image problem. It sounds like a technical discipline requiring an expensive subscription, and the tools are happy for you to keep believing that.

It is really four questions: what do people type, what do they want when they type it, can I realistically compete, and is it worth anything to me if I win. Every one of those can be answered for free — and the two that matter most cannot be answered by a tool at all.

[toc]

## Start with what you already rank for

If your site has been live for a few months, you have data nobody else has. Google Search Console → Performance → Queries shows the real searches that returned your pages.

Sort by impressions, then look for:

- **Searches on page two.** Positions 8–20 mean Google already considers you relevant. Improving an existing page is far cheaper than earning a new one.
- **High impressions, low clicks.** You are showing up and nobody is choosing you — usually a title problem, not a content problem.
- **Searches you never targeted.** These are gifts. If you rank accidentally for something, a page written deliberately for it will do better.

This pass alone usually produces a quarter's work, and it is the only source that describes your site rather than an average of everyone's.

## Expand with the sources Google gives you free

Autocomplete and the results page are built from real search behaviour. They are more honest about phrasing than any keyword tool.

- **Autocomplete.** Type your topic and watch the suggestions. Add a letter — "content calendar a", "content calendar b" — to fan out further.
- **People Also Ask.** The expandable questions on the results page. Each one is a heading for your article, and often a page of its own.
- **Related searches.** The block at the bottom of the results page.
- **Your own inbox.** The questions customers actually email you are searches somebody is making, phrased the way real people phrase them.

That last source is consistently the best and consistently the most ignored.

## Read the results page before you commit

This is the step people skip, and it decides everything.

Search your candidate term and read the first page properly. You are asking three things:

1. **What kind of page is winning?** All product pages means commercial intent — an explainer will not rank. All tutorials means the reverse. The results page is Google telling you what it thinks the search means.
2. **Who is winning?** Ten enormous brands with deep pages is a wall. A couple of thin blog posts and a forum thread is an opening.
3. **Is the current answer actually good?** If the top result answers the question badly, that is your opportunity. If it is comprehensive, current and well-structured, pick a different search.

[quote cite="The only test that matters"]
Could my page honestly be the best result on this page? If the answer is no, no amount of optimisation fixes it. If the answer is yes, most of the work is already done.
[/quote]

## Judge each candidate on four axes

[table caption="Volume is the least important column here."]
- Axis | Question | Weight
- **Intent match** | Does the searcher want what I actually offer? | Highest
- **Winnability** | Can my page be the best result? | High
- **Commercial value** | Does ranking here lead anywhere for my business? | High
- **Volume** | How many people search it? | Lowest
[/table]

Volume ranks last on purpose. Twenty searches a month from people ready to buy beat two thousand from people writing a school assignment. A small site that chases volume competes with everyone; a small site that chases specific, valuable intent competes with almost no one.

## Prefer the longer, more specific version

"Social media" is not a keyword, it is a subject. "How to schedule Instagram posts for multiple clients" is a keyword: specific, answerable, and searched by someone with a real problem.

Longer searches — long tail — are lower volume individually and better in every other way. Clearer intent, weaker competition, higher conversion, and they accumulate. Fifty specific pages each earning modest steady traffic is a much more reliable business than one page gambling on a head term.

They are also how you eventually earn the head term. Sites rank for broad, competitive searches after they have proven themselves on narrow ones.

## Group into topics, not lists

A spreadsheet of 400 keywords is not a plan. Group them.

Searches that want the same answer belong on one page. "Content calendar template", "how to make a content calendar" and "content calendar example" are one page. "Content calendar software" is a different page, because that searcher wants to buy something.

Each group becomes one page with one primary keyword and a handful of secondary phrasings. That grouping decision — which searches are the same question — is the actual skill, and no tool does it for you.

## Turn the groups into a queue

Order by commercial intent first, not by volume. Pages that catch people close to a decision earn their keep immediately; educational pages build authority slowly and are worth writing second.

Then work through the queue at a rate you can hold. The system for that is in [how to schedule content so it publishes itself](/resources/guides/how-to-schedule-content-so-it-publishes-itself), and the judgement about what a searcher wants is in [search intent and the four types](/resources/guides/search-intent-in-seo).

## When a paid tool is worth it

Not never — just not first. A subscription earns its cost when you can name the question it answers:

- You need competitor gap analysis at scale.
- You are managing enough sites that manual research is genuinely the bottleneck.
- You need historical trend data to justify a budget to someone else.

Until one of those is true, the free sources plus your own inbox will take you further than most people expect. The constraint on a new site is almost never the quality of its keyword list.
