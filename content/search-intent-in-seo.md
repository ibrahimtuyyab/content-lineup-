[answer]
Search intent is what someone actually wants when they type a query. There are four types: informational (learn), navigational (go to a specific site), commercial investigation (compare before buying), and transactional (buy or do now). You identify intent by reading the first page of results — whatever format dominates is what Google has already determined satisfies that search. Matching intent matters more than any on-page optimisation.
[/answer]

Here is the most expensive mistake in content marketing, and it is invisible while you make it: writing a genuinely excellent page that answers a question nobody was asking.

The words matched. The research was solid. The writing was good. It still never ranked, because the people typing that phrase wanted something structurally different from what you gave them — and Google, which has watched millions of people make that search, already knew.

## The four types

[table caption="What the searcher wants, and what ranks."]
- Intent | The searcher wants | What ranks | Example
- **Informational** | To understand something | Guides, explainers, how-tos | "what is a content calendar"
- **Navigational** | To reach a specific place | The official site, its login page | "buffer login"
- **Commercial** | To compare before deciding | Comparisons, "best of" lists, reviews | "best social media scheduler"
- **Transactional** | To act now | Product pages, pricing, sign-up | "buffer pricing"
[/table]

The boundary that matters most is between **informational** and **commercial**. It is where most businesses misjudge, and it is the difference between traffic that reads and traffic that buys.

## How to identify intent in about a minute

Do not reason about it from the words. Search it and read the page.

Google has already run the experiment. It has served results for that query to enormous numbers of people and watched which ones satisfied them. The format that dominates page one is not a hint — it is the conclusion.

Three things to check:

1. **What format is winning?** Ten how-to articles means informational. Ten product pages means transactional. Six comparison posts means commercial.
2. **What are the titles promising?** "How to", "what is", "guide" versus "best", "vs", "alternatives" versus "pricing", "buy", "free trial".
3. **What else is on the page?** A featured snippet and People Also Ask suggest informational. Shopping results and ads suggest transactional. A pack of review sites suggests commercial.

If your planned page does not match the dominant format, you have two options: change the page, or change the target. Publishing anyway is the third option and it does not work.

## The words lie more often than you would think

Query wording is an unreliable guide to intent. Some examples that catch people out:

- **"best free social media schedulers"** looks informational because of the word "free". It is commercial — the searcher is choosing a tool and will sign up for one today.
- **"content calendar template"** looks commercial. It is mostly informational-with-a-download; the results are articles offering a free template, not products.
- **"instagram automation"** is genuinely mixed, which is why it is hard. Some searchers want to know what is possible, others want a tool.

This is why the results page beats intuition every time. It costs a minute and it is right.

## What to publish for each intent

**Informational.** Answer the question directly in the first screenful, then go deeper than the current top result. These pages build authority and get quoted by AI answer engines, but they rarely convert immediately — so link them to the pages that do.

**Navigational.** If it is your own brand, own the page and make sure your login and pricing URLs are indexable. If it is someone else's brand, the only honest play is a comparison, and it needs to be genuinely fair to be worth reading.

**Commercial.** This is the highest-value content most businesses under-invest in. Comparisons, alternatives lists, honest reviews. The rule that makes them work: say plainly where the competitor wins. A comparison where you win every row convinces nobody, because nobody believes it.

**Transactional.** Your product and pricing pages. Keep them fast, keep the price visible, and remove anything between the reader and the action.

## Mixed intent, and how to handle it

Sometimes page one is genuinely split — two guides, two product pages, a video. Google has not settled on a single interpretation.

These are difficult but winnable, because nobody else has fully satisfied the search either. The approach that works: cover the dominant intent thoroughly, then address the secondary one in a clearly-signposted section rather than blending them into mush. A guide with an explicit "if you are looking for a tool" section can satisfy both without confusing either.

## Where this connects to the rest of SEO

Intent is the filter you apply during [keyword research](/resources/guides/how-to-perform-keyword-research), before you commit to writing anything. It is also the reason two pages targeting near-identical words can both be right — they are answering different questions.

## SEO and content marketing are not the same job

A useful distinction, since intent is where they meet. SEO is about being findable — the technical and structural work that lets a search engine understand and trust a page. Content marketing is about being worth finding — having something to say that earns attention once someone arrives.

You need both, and they fail differently. Content marketing without SEO produces good work nobody discovers. SEO without content marketing produces discoverable pages nobody values, which stop ranking as soon as a competitor publishes something better.

Intent is where the two meet: it is simultaneously a search engine signal and a straightforward description of what your reader wanted.
