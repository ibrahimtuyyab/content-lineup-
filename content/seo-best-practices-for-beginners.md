[answer]
Start with the four things that compound: make sure Google can crawl and index your site, pick one clear topic per page, write pages that fully answer the search behind them, and get your titles and internal links right. Everything else — link building, schema, Core Web Vitals tuning — is worth doing later and worth nothing if those four are broken. Expect three to six months before results are visible.
[/answer]

Most SEO writing has a problem: it is written by people who do SEO all day, for people who do SEO all day. If you run a business and simply want your site to show up, most of it reads like weather forecasting in a language you do not speak.

This guide is the other version. Ten things, in the order they are worth doing, with the reasoning attached so you can tell when a rule stops applying.

[toc]

## What SEO actually is, in one paragraph

A search engine has one job: given a search, return the page most likely to satisfy the person who typed it. SEO is the work of making your page the honest answer to that question — and making sure nothing technical stops the search engine from finding it, reading it, and believing it.

That framing kills a lot of bad advice on contact. "Will this help the person who searched?" answers almost every SEO question you will have. If a tactic only makes sense as a trick played on software, it is either useless now or about to be.

## The three types of SEO, and which one you need first

SEO gets split into three areas. They are not equally urgent for a small site.

[table caption="Where a small business gets the most return, in order."]
- Type | What it covers | How much it matters early
- **On-page** | Content, headings, page titles, internal links, images | Most of your result. Start here.
- **Technical** | Crawling, indexing, site speed, mobile rendering, sitemaps | Matters only when broken — but when broken, nothing else works.
- **Off-page** | Links and mentions from other sites, reputation | Real, slow, and mostly earned by the first two.
[/table]

The common beginner mistake is starting with off-page — chasing links for a site with four thin pages. Links pointing at a page that does not answer anything do very little. Build something worth linking to first.

## 1. Make sure you are actually indexed

Before anything else: search `site:yourdomain.com` in Google. If your pages are not listed, nothing else in this guide matters yet.

Then set up [Google Search Console](https://search.google.com/search-console). It is free, it takes ten minutes, and it is the only source that tells you what your site really ranks for rather than a third-party estimate. Verify the domain, submit your sitemap, and check the Pages report for anything marked "not indexed" with a reason attached.

## 2. Give every page one job

One page, one topic, one primary search. This is the single highest-return habit in this guide.

When two pages target the same search, you split your own signals and the search engine picks one — usually not the one you wanted. When one page tries to cover four topics, it ranks strongly for none of them because it fully satisfies nobody.

If you already have overlapping pages, the fix is usually to merge them into one better page and redirect the loser to the winner.

## 3. Work out what the searcher actually wants

The same words can mean completely different things. Someone searching "content calendar" might want a definition, a template to download, or software to buy. If you write a buying guide for a search where everyone wants a free template, you will not rank no matter how good the writing is.

This is called search intent, and getting it wrong is the most common reason a genuinely good page fails. It has its own guide: [search intent and the four types](/resources/guides/search-intent-in-seo).

## 4. Find searches you can realistically win

There is no point targeting a search dominated by sites a thousand times your size. Look for questions with clear intent, obvious commercial relevance to you, and results currently answered by something mediocre.

You do not need a paid tool for this. The full method is in [how to do keyword research without a paid tool](/resources/guides/how-to-perform-keyword-research).

## 5. Answer the question in the first screenful

Put the direct answer near the top, in plain language, before the context and the caveats. Three reasons:

- Readers decide in seconds whether to stay. A page that makes them scroll to find out whether they are in the right place loses many of them.
- Search engines pull featured snippets from clear, self-contained answers.
- AI answer engines quote passages that stand alone. A paragraph that only makes sense after three paragraphs of preamble does not get quoted.

You can still write the long version. Just do not make it a prerequisite.

## 6. Get the title and description right

Your page title is the single most important on-page element, and it does two jobs: it tells the search engine what the page is about, and it convinces a human to click.

- Keep it roughly 50–60 characters so it is not cut off.
- Put the primary phrase near the front, without contorting the sentence.
- Make every title on your site different from every other.
- Write the meta description as ad copy, not a summary. It does not affect ranking directly, but it affects whether anyone clicks — and that is the point.

## 7. Use headings as a structure, not decoration

One `H1` per page — normally the title. `H2`s for main sections, `H3`s nested underneath. Do not skip levels for visual effect; use CSS if you want smaller text.

Question-shaped headings work unusually well, because they match how people search and they make the page easy for an answer engine to break into extractable pieces.

## 8. Link your own pages together deliberately

Internal links are the most underused tool available to a small site. They pass authority between your own pages, and they tell search engines which pages you consider important.

Two rules that cover most cases:

- Every guide should link **up** to whichever product or service page owns the same topic.
- Every product page should link **down** to at least one guide that explains it.

Use descriptive link text. "Learn more" tells a search engine nothing; "how to schedule content so it publishes itself" tells it everything.

## 9. Handle images properly

Images are where technical debt accumulates quietly.

- Write real alt text — what the image shows, for someone who cannot see it. Not a keyword dump.
- Compress before uploading. A 4 MB photo straight from a phone is the most common cause of a slow page.
- Set width and height so the layout does not jump while loading.
- Use descriptive filenames: `heat-pump-vs-furnace.jpg`, not `IMG_4821.jpg`.

## 10. Publish consistently, then leave things alone

SEO compounds. Ten solid pages published steadily over ten months beat forty published in one week and then abandoned — partly because a site that keeps publishing looks alive, and mostly because you learn what works and apply it to the next one.

The counterpart is patience. Rewriting a page every fortnight because it has not ranked in three weeks destroys your ability to tell what worked. Publish, wait a quarter, check Search Console, then change one thing.

[quote cite="The rule of thumb"]
If you cannot sustain your publishing plan through your busiest month, it is not a plan, it is an intention. Pick the cadence that survives a bad month.
[/quote]

## What to ignore for now

Not wrong, just not yet:

- **Link building campaigns.** Earn links with useful pages first.
- **Schema markup beyond the basics.** Helpful, rarely decisive early.
- **Chasing perfect Core Web Vitals scores.** Fix genuinely slow pages; ignore the last ten points.
- **Keyword density tools.** The metric has not mattered for over a decade.
- **Daily rank tracking.** Positions bounce. Watch monthly trends in Search Console instead.

## A realistic first ninety days

[table caption="A sequence that fits around an actual job."]
- Weeks | Focus
- 1–2 | Search Console set up, sitemap submitted, indexing problems fixed
- 3–4 | Audit existing pages: one topic each, titles rewritten, duplicates merged
- 5–8 | Keyword research; pick six topics you can genuinely win
- 9–12 | Publish the first four, link them to each other and to your service pages
[/table]

After that it is repetition: publish, link, wait, check, adjust. The tenth article is dramatically easier than the first, and worth more, because it lands on a site the search engines already trust a little.

## Where content marketing fits

SEO and content marketing are related but not the same job — SEO is about being findable, content marketing is about being worth finding. Most small businesses need both, and the overlap is large enough that the distinction only matters when you are deciding where to spend a limited week.
